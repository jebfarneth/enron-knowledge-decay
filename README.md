# Hidden Functional Importance in Workplace Email (Enron)

*Can language and communication patterns in email measure how much an
organization depends on a person, and where that differs from their formal rank?*

## Research question

Prior NLP work predicts **formal hierarchy** from email (who outranks whom).
This project aims to treat formal rank and **functional importance** (how much
others depend on a person) as separate quantities, measure both from the
Enron corpus, and study where they diverge. Validating functional importance
(for example against what happened to a person's contacts after they left) is
a planned later phase; nothing in the current code measures it yet.

## Phase 1 result: data layer

![From raw corpus to analysis set](figures/fig01_data_funnel.png)

| Step | Messages |
|---|---:|
| Files in the corpus | 517,401 |
| Dated 1998–2002 | 516,359 |
| After removing duplicate copies (233 same-text messages to disjoint recipient lists kept as candidate separate sends) | 254,110 |
| With estimated authored text | 234,295 |
| Analysis set: internal senders, excluding 9,429 automated messages, 6,434 structured records and copied newsletters, 1,026 signature-only messages, 3,313 long routine messages and 1,531 probable time-shifted copies | 168,510 (6,326 sender addresses) |
| Person text: analysis messages whose sender is attributed to a person | 165,431 (5,508 people) |

Authored text is an estimate: quoted material the cleaner misses stays in,
and answers written inline inside a quote are lost. All flags are
heuristics, not a validated classifier. A message is routine
when its sender repeats the whole text; repeated short messages ("approved",
"please print", "will do") are speech acts and stay in the analysis set.
Signature-only messages (an optional sign-off, then a line with the
sender's first and last name followed only by title, department, address and
phone lines) are excluded; audit 5 found 3 of 60 sampled exclusions to be
real content before the rule was tightened; signatures inside ordinary messages
are still present and must be removed before any text model (Phase 3).

Senders are attributed per message from the message's own display name. The
6,455 internal sender addresses resolve to 5,871 keys (5,596 people, 145
roles, 127 addresses with no usable name, 2 lists, 1 ambiguous); four
placeholder addresses are never treated as a person. Role keys include
numbered, shared and departmental mailboxes in every header rendering.
Aliases merge on shared directory IDs or a go-by middle name, and names with
two well-supported middle initials split (Mark A and Mark E Taylor).
"Person" and "person text" are operational labels from these rules, not
verified identities.

Reply links are inferred between people and need direct evidence: the reply
is addressed back to the earlier sender or quotes it. When the first quoted
header the parser recognizes names an author (a From: line or a Lotus Notes
header), only that author's messages can be the parent, even if an older
message is quoted further down, and a message whose first recognized quote
is its own sender's is left unlinked. A link is a reply when it is addressed back and its subject is not
a forward (FW:), otherwise a forward. Each link is graded: high when the
quoted author and quoted text both point to the parent, medium when one
does, low when the message is only addressed back. These are evidence
categories, not calibrated probabilities: in audit 5's sample, 18 of 20
high, 12 of 20 medium and 15 of 20 low links were direct replies. The
stricter rules also dropped real replies: of the 2,282 reply links removed
after audit 4, audit 5 estimates about 1,065 (690 to 1,457) were genuine. There are 34,762 links:
26,902 replies and 7,860 forwards; 26,452 links are high confidence. On the
first audit's 60 labelled links, 24 of the 27 true direct replies are still
linked as replies, and 24 of the 28 sampled links that keep their parent and
are labelled replies (85.7%) are true direct replies; that sample informed
the rules, so it is a regression check. Audit 4's fresh random sample
(before these changes) found 47 of 60 reply links to be direct replies.
Links remain inferred, not observed.

## Phase 2 result: network baselines

![Which measure recovers formal rank?](figures/fig06_baselines_formal_rank.png)

Two label sets are used. Accuracy is the share of pairs a measure orders
correctly (chance = 50%), with 95% intervals from resampling people.

**Title proxy.** Seniority levels (CEO 6 … trader/employee 0) for 129 people
in the Shetty & Adibi (2004) title list: a noisy, undated proxy, not ground
truth. Two duplicate rows with conflicting titles are dropped following
Diesner & Carley (2005), and four labels that the list's notes or the
person's signature call into question are flagged. 6,235 different-level
pairs.

**Gold standard.** The Agarwal et al. (2012) hierarchy, from a release the
authors shared privately (request it from them). Its org-chart positions give
13,241 dominance pairs among 1,518 employees; this reconstruction does not
reproduce the paper's counts (13,724), so results are related to the
paper's, not a replication. Employees are matched to graph nodes by their
principal name (release records for executives often list their assistants'
addresses). The main population is the 7,338 pairs whose two employees are
matched and whose dominance does not start, end or run through one of the
eight records that merge an assistant's position with another.

The graph has 20,755 nodes: 5,563 people, 140 roles, 305 distribution lists,
2 ambiguous keys and 14,745 internal addresses that could not be matched to a
person.

| Measure | Title proxy (6,235 pairs) | Gold standard (7,338 pairs) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Others mentioned to them (mention network) | 69.5% (62.0–75.9) | **92.2%** (87.5–94.9) |
| People mentioned to them (mention network, primary) | 69.4% (62.0–75.7) | 91.3% (86.0–94.4) |
| Mention-network degree | **73.4%** (66.5–79.6) | 90.5% (83.4–94.7) |
| PageRank | 62.9% (55.7–70.1) | 91.7% (85.9–95.1) |
| Degree (distinct contacts) | 66.6% (59.0–73.4) | 89.6% (82.5–93.8) |
| Email received (weighted) | 62.3% (55.4–68.7) | 88.8% (82.5–93.1) |
| Betweenness (exact) | 58.3% (50.5–65.6) | 79.0% (67.4–87.4) |
| Email sent (messages) | 52.9% (44.7–60.1) | 75.7% (67.6–83.1) |
| Custodian baseline (mailbox in the corpus) | — | 65.6% (53.5–77.2) |

Among the email-only network measures, degree has the highest title-proxy point
estimate; on the gold standard PageRank leads degree by 2.1 points (paired
interval 0.5 to 4.7). Title-proxy sensitivity runs move degree between 61.6%
(CEOs and presidents removed) and 68.3% (people-only graph); graphs built
only from messages with at most 10 recipients give 63.9%, so broadcasts
account for part of degree's signal. On the gold standard, whether a
person's mailbox was collected alone orders 94.2% of pairs between a
custodian and a non-custodian, close to degree's 95.5%, so that part of the
task largely reflects corpus collection. A few executives appear in
thousands of pairs: weighting each manager equally gives degree 76.3%, and
dropping Kenneth Lay and Jeffrey Skilling gives 85.8%. Excluding also the
records whose addresses belong to several people (a stricter population of
4,939 pairs) gives degree 92.9%; all constructions and scorings are in
[`results/gold_standard_sensitivity.csv`](results/gold_standard_sensitivity.csv).
For comparison, raw-address degree over the windowed, deduplicated mail
gives 83.1% on all 13,241 pairs (the paper reports 83.88% on its own pairs
with an alias-merged graph).

## First language signal: the mention network

Agarwal, Omuya, Zhang & Rambow (2014) found that *how many different people
are mentioned to you* predicts rank better than the email network. The
`mentions` stage adapts their measure (it is not a replication: spaCy's
`en_core_web_sm` replaces their AceJet tagger, the graph and candidates
differ, and equal-distance ties abstain). Person names are tagged in the
first 5,000 characters of each message's estimated authored text, and each
name resolves, per recipient, to the compatible person closest to both
sender and recipient in the email network. The main measures count only
person-text messages and leave out every name that resolves to the sender
(mostly signatures and contact lines, but also rosters and mixed contexts:
323,002 of 988,281 resolved mentions), names after an office title on the same
line ("Governor Davis", 6,019) and names before a company word on the same
line ("Williams pipeline", 278). People mentioned to them was declared
the primary mention measure after the unfiltered results were known but
before the filters were run, so the filtered comparison is exploratory; the
unfiltered definition is kept for comparison. In audit 5's sample of 60 kept
mentions, 43 pointed to the right person, 11 to the wrong one and 6 were
uncertain (for example Gray Davis resolved to an Enron Davis).

The paper's Cc check (when exactly one compatible person is Cc'd, take them
as the answer) agrees with the resolver on 70.7% of 19,037 cases: 84.7% when
the name fits one person and 60.4% when it fits several. The resolver uses
the same email network, Cc edges included, so this is a consistency check,
not an independent accuracy (the paper reports 69.7% on its own graph).

| Measure (gold standard, 7,338 pairs) | Accuracy | Minus degree (paired 95% interval) |
|---|---:|---:|
| People mentioned to them (primary) | 91.3% | +1.7 (−0.4 to +4.9) |
| Others mentioned to them (sender and recipients excluded) | 92.2% | +2.7 (+0.6 to +5.9) |
| Mention-network degree | 90.5% | +0.9 (−1.2 to +2.9) |
| People mentioned to them, unfiltered | 92.2% | +2.7 (+0.7 to +5.4) |

With the filters, the primary measure's gain over degree is no longer
distinguishable from zero on the main gold population; it is ahead of degree
in every construction and scoring of the sensitivity runs (by 0.3 to 3.7
points) and on gold non-core pairs, whose employees' mailboxes were not
collected (89.8% against 86.2%). On the title proxy, mention-network degree
minus degree is +6.8 points (+2.8 to +11.2) and people mentioned to them
minus degree is +2.8 (−0.9 to +6.9). Rosters, forwarded news and quoted headers still contribute
names, and a person key is not verified employment. This is the first signal
derived from what people write rather than whom they write to; Phase 3 text
models must beat it on the same pairs.

## Independent check of the cleaning

Quote removal was compared with `email_reply_parser` (Zapier's Python port of
GitHub's reply parser) on 5,000 messages sampled with a seed not used while
writing the rules. The two outputs agree after whitespace normalization on
77.2% of messages. Neither tool is ground truth, and marker counts alone
reward deleting text, so an empty cleaner is shown as a control:

| On 5,000 sampled messages | This pipeline | email_reply_parser | Empty cleaner |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Output empty | 7.4% | 3.5% | 100% |
| "Original Message" separator left | 0.08% | 2.0% | 0% |
| "Forwarded by" banner left | 0.04% | 6.0% | 0% |
| Quoted header line left | 1.0% | 7.8% | 0% |

Most of the extra empty outputs are messages that only forward with a Lotus
Notes banner. Answers written inline inside a quoted message are still lost,
and signatures and pasted documents inside messages remain.

## Reproduce

```sh
make all           # uv sync, unit tests, every stage in order, then real-corpus checks
```

A first run takes about 1.5 hours on an 8 GB machine (name tagging is cached
after that). Tunable parameters live in [`config.yaml`](config.yaml); fixed
rules (regular expressions, word lists, folder priorities) are versioned in
the source. The corpus is the CMU release of May 7, 2015
(<https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~enron/>), checked against a pinned SHA-256 on every
run. The parsed cache is reused only when its stamp matches the archive, the
parser source, Python, the lockfile and the cached table's checksum. Every
stage records the checksums of its declared outputs and of the files it
reads besides earlier outputs (raw corpus, title list, gold release,
labelled thread sample, NER model files, `uv.lock`), with the code and
configuration hashes and the Python version, in
`data/processed/manifest.json`; the real-corpus checks pass only for one
complete run, every stage built by the current code, configuration, Python
and inputs after the stages it reads, with every declared output unchanged.
Cached name tags are reused only when each name sits at its recorded
position in the text. Without the private release the two gold-standard stages are
recorded as skipped, delete their earlier processed files and result tables,
and say so. Dependencies are pinned in
`uv.lock` for Python 3.12; runs have been reproduced on macOS only.
