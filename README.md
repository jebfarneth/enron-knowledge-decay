# Hidden Functional Importance in Workplace Email (Enron)

*Can language and communication patterns in email measure how much an
organization depends on a person, and where that differs from their formal rank?*

## Research question

Prior NLP work predicts **formal hierarchy** from email (who outranks whom).
This project aims to treat formal rank and **functional importance** (how much
others depend on a person) as separate quantities, measure both from the
Enron corpus, and study where they diverge. Validating functional importance
(for example against what happened to a person's contacts after they left) is
a planned later phase; nothing in the current code measures it yet.

## Phase 1 result: data layer

![From raw corpus to analysis set](figures/fig01_data_funnel.png)

| Step | Messages |
|---|---:|
| Files in the corpus | 517,401 |
| Dated 1998–2002 | 516,359 |
| After removing duplicate copies (233 same-text messages to disjoint recipient lists kept as candidate separate sends) | 254,110 |
| With estimated authored text | 234,296 |
| Analysis set: internal senders, excluding 9,429 automated messages, 8,639 structured records and copied newsletters, 1,366 signature-only messages, 3,313 long routine messages and 1,531 probable time-shifted copies | 168,170 (6,320 sender addresses) |
| Person text: analysis messages whose sender is attributed to a person | 165,105 (5,504 people) |

All flags are heuristics, not a validated classifier. A message is routine
when its sender repeats the whole text; repeated short messages ("approved",
"please print", "will do") are speech acts and stay in the analysis set.
Signature-only messages (a name line followed only by title, department,
address and phone lines) are excluded; signatures inside ordinary messages
are still present and must be removed before any text model (Phase 3).

Senders are attributed per message from the message's own display name. The
6,455 internal sender addresses resolve to 5,871 keys (5,596 people, 145
roles, 127 addresses with no usable name, 2 lists, 1 ambiguous); four
placeholder addresses are never treated as a person. Role keys include
numbered, shared and departmental mailboxes in every header rendering.
Aliases merge on shared directory IDs or a go-by middle name, and names with
two well-supported middle initials split (Mark A and Mark E Taylor).
"Person" and "person text" are operational labels from these rules, not
verified identities.

Reply links are inferred between people and need direct evidence: the reply
is addressed back to the earlier sender or quotes it, and when its quoted
header names an author, the parent must be that author's message. There are
37,493 links: 28,989 replies and 8,504 forwards. On the first audit's 60
labelled links, 24 of the 27 true direct replies are still linked as
replies, and 24 of the 31 sampled links that keep their parent and are
labelled replies (77.4%) are true direct replies; that sample informed the
rules, so it is a regression check. A fresh random
sample in audit 3 (before the latest changes) found 50 of 60 reply links to
be direct replies. Links remain inferred, not observed.

## Phase 2 result: network baselines

![Which measure recovers formal rank?](figures/fig06_baselines_formal_rank.png)

Two label sets are used. Accuracy is the share of pairs a measure orders
correctly (chance = 50%), with 95% intervals from resampling people.

**Title proxy.** Seniority levels (CEO 6 … trader/employee 0) for 129 people
in the Shetty & Adibi (2004) title list: a noisy, undated proxy, not ground
truth. Two duplicate rows with conflicting titles are dropped following
Diesner & Carley (2005), and four labels that the list's notes or the
person's signature call into question are flagged. 6,235 different-level
pairs.

**Gold standard.** The Agarwal et al. (2012) hierarchy, from a release the
authors shared privately (request it from them). Its org-chart positions give
13,241 dominance pairs among 1,518 employees; this reconstruction does not
reproduce the paper's counts (13,724), so results are related to the
paper's, not a replication. Employees are matched to graph nodes by their
principal name (release records for executives often list their assistants'
addresses). The main population is the 7,338 pairs whose two employees are
matched and whose dominance does not start, end or run through one of the
eight records that merge an assistant's position with another.

The graph has 20,752 nodes: 5,562 people, 139 roles, 305 distribution lists,
2 ambiguous keys and 14,744 internal addresses that could not be matched to a
person.

| Measure | Title proxy (6,235 pairs) | Gold standard (7,338 pairs) |
|---|---:|---:|
| People mentioned to them (mention network) | 70.4% (63.1–76.6) | **92.2%** (86.9–95.2) |
| Mention-network degree | **73.1%** (66.3–79.1) | 90.5% (83.4–94.7) |
| PageRank | 62.9% (55.7–70.1) | 91.7% (85.9–95.1) |
| Degree (distinct contacts) | 66.6% (59.0–73.4) | 89.6% (82.5–93.8) |
| Email received (weighted) | 62.3% (55.4–68.7) | 88.8% (82.5–93.1) |
| Betweenness (exact) | 58.3% (50.5–65.6) | 79.0% (67.4–87.4) |
| Email sent (messages) | 52.9% (44.7–60.1) | 75.7% (67.6–83.1) |
| Custodian baseline (mailbox in the corpus) | — | 65.6% (53.5–77.2) |

Among the network measures, degree has the highest title-proxy point
estimate; on the gold standard PageRank leads degree by 2.1 points (paired
interval 0.5 to 4.7). Title-proxy sensitivity runs move degree between 61.6%
(CEOs and presidents removed) and 68.3% (people-only graph); graphs built
only from messages with at most 10 recipients give 63.9%, so broadcasts
account for part of degree's signal. On the gold standard, whether a
person's mailbox was collected alone orders 94.2% of pairs between a
custodian and a non-custodian, close to degree's 95.5%, so that part of the
task largely reflects corpus collection. A few executives appear in
thousands of pairs: weighting each manager equally gives degree 76.3%, and
dropping Kenneth Lay and Jeffrey Skilling gives 85.8%. Excluding also the
records whose addresses belong to several people (a stricter population of
4,939 pairs) gives degree 92.9%; all constructions and scorings are in
[`results/gold_standard_sensitivity.csv`](results/gold_standard_sensitivity.csv).
For comparison, raw-address degree over the windowed, deduplicated mail
gives 83.1% on all 13,241 pairs (the paper reports 83.88% on its own pairs
with an alias-merged graph).

## First language signal: the mention network

Agarwal, Omuya, Zhang & Rambow (2014) found that *how many different people
are mentioned to you* predicts rank better than the email network. The
`mentions` stage rebuilds their measure: spaCy's named-entity recognizer
finds person names in each message's authored text, and each name resolves
to the compatible person closest to both sender and recipient in the email
network, as in the paper (ties abstain). Resolution agrees with the paper's
Cc-based check on 70.7% of cases (the paper reports 69.7%).

Mention measures beat degree on both label sets: people mentioned to them
+2.6 points on the gold standard (paired interval +0.7 to +5.5), and
mention-network degree +6.5 on the title proxy (+3.2 to +10.1). The gain is
largest for employees whose own mailboxes were not collected (gold non-core
pairs: 90.5% against 86.2%). This is the first signal derived from what
people write rather than whom they write to; Phase 3 text models must beat
it on the same pairs.

## Independent check of the cleaning

Quote removal was compared with `email_reply_parser` (Zapier's Python port of
GitHub's reply parser) on 5,000 messages sampled with a seed not used while
writing the rules. The two outputs agree after whitespace normalization on
77.2% of messages. Neither tool is ground truth, and marker counts alone
reward deleting text, so an empty cleaner is shown as a control:

| On 5,000 sampled messages | This pipeline | email_reply_parser | Empty cleaner |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Output empty | 7.4% | 3.5% | 100% |
| "Original Message" separator left | 0.08% | 2.0% | 0% |
| "Forwarded by" banner left | 0.04% | 6.0% | 0% |
| Quoted header line left | 1.0% | 7.8% | 0% |

Most of the extra empty outputs are messages that only forward with a Lotus
Notes banner. Answers written inline inside a quoted message are still lost,
and signatures and pasted documents inside messages remain.

## Reproduce

```sh
make all           # uv sync, unit tests, every stage in order, then real-corpus checks
```

A first run takes about 1.5 hours on an 8 GB machine (name tagging is cached
after that). Tunable parameters live in [`config.yaml`](config.yaml); fixed
rules (regular expressions, word lists, folder priorities) are versioned in
the source. The corpus is the CMU release of May 7, 2015
(<https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~enron/>), checked against a pinned SHA-256 on every
run. The parsed cache is reused only when its stamp matches the archive, the
parser source, Python, the lockfile and the cached table's checksum;
`funnel.json` records the code and configuration hashes, and the real-corpus
checks fail when either, or any output file, no longer matches. The
gold-standard stages need the private release and delete their outputs and
report that they were skipped without it. Dependencies are pinned in
`uv.lock` for Python 3.12; runs have been reproduced on macOS only.
